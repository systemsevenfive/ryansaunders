# Site suggestions — ryansaunders.ca

## What the site is doing

It signals that you finish things, at a volume that's actually unusual. The Shipped / Tools / Lab / On Paper structure is a real maturity ladder; most dev portfolios don't have a structure that admits ideas exist at different stages of cooked. The footer line about light mode does more work than it should — it tells a reader you have taste, voice, and that you're not going to be tedious in a meeting. That's a hiring signal LLMs cannot generate.

## What it isn't doing

The hero positions you exactly in the middle of the pack:

> Senior iOS developer shipping finished apps to solve real problems. Embracing AI to explore everything that used to be impractical.

That sentence could belong to ten thousand other devs in 2026. Your actual bets — Apple Foundation Models / on-device LLM work, the Claude Code refinery, Sonny — are either buried below the fold or absent from the homepage entirely. The site presents you as "iOS developer who uses AI." The story you're living is "iOS developer working at an edge most of the field hasn't reached yet." Different noun, different modifier, completely different hireability.

The site also has no writing. For the lane you want to be in — the one that doesn't filter through LeetCode — writing is most of how people decide whether to bring you in. One serious post would shift the site from "competent portfolio" to "this person is worth paying attention to."

## The core move

Reposition from *iOS developer who uses AI* to *iOS developer working at the AI-native edge*.

This is one positional shift that propagates through the whole site:

- The hero changes
- Sonny gets promoted out of the Tools bucket
- An on-device / Foundation Models section appears
- The Refinery work becomes visible
- The Lab gets trimmed to projects that reinforce the edge story

The hero rewrite isn't cosmetic. It determines what the rest of the site has to do.

## Specific changes, in priority order

### 1. Rewrite the hero

Drop "Senior iOS developer" as the leading noun. Lead with what only you do.

A direction (not the answer):

> iOS developer working at the edge of on-device intelligence. Shipping in Swift. Designing systems that turn AI into leverage.

The point is to put your two real bets in the first sentence. Whatever copy you land on, it needs to do that.

Possible second line that matches your voice better than the current one:

> Solo on shipped apps, opinionated on tools, currently very interested in what Apple's Foundation Models change about what a small team can build alone.

### 2. Promote Sonny

Sonny is your most defensible long-term project. Tooling for understanding code that AI agents write is a growth category, not a maintenance one. Right now it sits next to Nexus, framed as a personal utility. That undersells it.

Consider:

- Pulling Sonny out of the Tools bucket and giving it its own slot
- Reframing it from "maps Swift codebases" toward something closer to "navigate and verify code at agent speed"
- Adding a screenshot or short demo so the value reads at a glance

### 3. Add an Edge / On-Device section

Whatever your Foundation Models work currently is — even a single rough prototype — show it. Right now there is zero on-site evidence that you do this work. The hedge you described to me is invisible to anyone evaluating you.

Even a stub is better than the current absence:

> **Foundation Models** — exploring on-device inference with Apple's local LLMs. Notes and prototypes posted as I find what's possible.

It establishes the territory. You can populate it over time.

### 4. Add a Writing section with one post

The single highest-leverage move on this list.

The first post writes itself: how you actually run your Claude Code refinery. Setup, ticket structure, where AI is good and where it falls down, what changed when you went from 5x to 20x, what you'd do differently. People are actively searching for this content and you have ground truth most people writing about it don't.

You don't need a blog platform. One MD file rendered on the site is enough to start. The second post can wait until the first one tells you what you actually want to write about next.

### 5. Make hiring you easier

GitHub link, resume download, and one line of intent. Right now the site shows what you make but doesn't tell anyone how to bring you in. Suggested addition, somewhere visible:

> Available for full-time, contract, or short-engagement work on iOS, on-device AI, or AI-augmented dev workflows.

Adjust to what you're actually open to.

### 6. Trim the Lab to three

Five in-flight reads slightly scattered. Pick the three that most reinforce the edge story and let the rest sit in On Paper until they're closer.

Suggested keep:

- **Ed Assists** — leans on the film background, makes the unusual cross-domain combination concrete
- **LCARS** — shows craft and visual sensibility, makes the on-device home story tangible
- **Neon Arcade** *or* **Chartula** — pick one for variety

ShopRoute and whichever of the two you don't pick can move to On Paper without losing anything. Five projects with one moving forward each quarter looks scattered; three with visible momentum looks intentional.

## What to preserve

- The footer line about light mode. Don't sand this off.
- The counter format (`3 Shipped · 2 Tools · 5 In the lab · 42 On Paper`). It's distinctive and dry.
- The general design restraint. Resist gradients, testimonials, "Let's build something amazing together," and pitch-deck phrases.
- The taxonomy. Shipped / Tools / Lab / On Paper is rare and good.
- The voice generally. The site is dryly self-aware in a way most dev portfolios aren't, and that's worth more than another section.

## What I'd skip from the other recommendations

- **Title inflation.** "Principal Engineer" / "Systems Architect" overshoots what you'd defend in a room and clashes hard with your voice. The cost of being clocked as inflated is much higher than the benefit of looking senior.
- **Forced analogies.** The DIT-to-AI-engineer parallel sounds clever and isn't meaningful. The value of your film background is taste and the cross-domain combination, not data-pipeline-pattern-matching.
- **Section rebrands as pitch-deck.** "Product Lab: Native Intelligence" / "Hardware-Intimate Computing" replace your current restraint with the vocabulary that makes most portfolio sites unreadable.
- **Retroactive progression narratives.** Don't restructure your apps as a tidy ladder of increasing complexity. Real portfolios rarely have neat arcs and manufacturing one looks fake.
- **Cost-aware as positioning.** Local-first is a value proposition. "Cost-aware" is a hedge for you, not a benefit for anyone reading the site.

## One more thing

The deeper move underneath all of this is to be more specific, not more impressive. Narrow until the people who want exactly what you do recognize you instantly, and accept that everyone else won't. Most portfolio advice — including some of what you've gotten — points the other direction: add more, dress up the language, broaden the appeal. That advice is usually wrong, and especially wrong now, when generic competence is exactly what's being commoditized.

The site is undersold but it isn't generic. Undersold is the easier of those two problems to fix.
